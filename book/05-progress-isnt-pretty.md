# 5. Progress Isn't Pretty

## Mastery Is Repetition with Feedback

Practice doesn't make perfect. Perfect practice makes improvements.

That is what separates a hundred wasted hours from a hundred that actually move you forward. The reps you log have to be deliberate, they have to be honest, and they have to teach you something the next time you show up.

You can see it clearly in cybersecurity, where penetration tests are our version of reps under pressure: simulated real-world attacks run across teams, infrastructure, and products. We don't go easy, and we don't telegraph the punch. Sometimes we don't even tell the blue team it's happening, because defenders don't get a warning in the real world, and if we want to build muscle memory we need to simulate the actual weight. That is what *perfect practice* really means: not pristine, flawless action, but high-quality, high-context repetition.

Malcolm Gladwell's *10,000 Hour Rule*, popularized in *Outliers*, offers a compelling headline: do something for 10,000 hours and you'll become one of the best in the world at it. What often gets forgotten, and what Gladwell himself has acknowledged, is that the number only matters if those hours are deliberate, structured, and improving. It is not just any reps that get you there, it is the right reps, and that holds in lifting, in product, and in leadership.

### The Lifting Lens: Periodization and Progression

Walk into any well-run gym and you'll see programs: 5/3/1, the Texas Method, Juggernaut, Conjugate. Each one offers structure, progression, and intentional overload, and each is built on periodization, which just means you cycle through intensities, movements, and recovery phases on purpose. It isn't sexy, but it works.

Chad Wesley Smith, founder of Juggernaut Training Systems, built JTS by emphasizing smart lifting over heavy lifting. As he puts it:

> *"Motivation gets you started. Discipline keeps you going. Feedback tells you where to go next."*
> — Chad Wesley Smith, founder of Juggernaut Training Systems

I use the JuggernautAI app myself. It takes in feedback on how I move, how I recover, and how I progress, then adjusts the plan accordingly, so every session has a purpose. It is a coach in your pocket, and a reminder that great outcomes come from smart, consistent reps.

Lifters learn something else the hard way: showing up and doing something beats waiting for the perfect plan. A light session with focused movement and intention will beat a skipped workout every time, and small improvements made consistently compound into real strength.

### The Product Mirror: Disciplined Iteration at Elastic

When Elastic Security shipped its initial security analytics and SIEM product in 2019, it wasn't a complete answer to the market. It was a start, informed by users, opinionated in structure, and designed to evolve. What made it successful wasn't just the launch, it was the loop: weekly user feedback, signal from real-world deployments, and disciplined iteration sprints that followed a clear roadmap.

Each product cycle was a planned lift, part of a broader program of growth, not a shot in the dark. Features like detection rules, case management, and timeline didn't just "appear." They were reps, refined under load. We looked at how users engaged, what didn't click, and what needed retraining, and we built telemetry into the stack, not to track vanity, but to see where improvement was possible.

The discipline scales beyond security. Watch how Anthropic has rolled out Claude from 2023 through 2026: a deliberate cadence of model versions, capability updates documented in public changelogs, and a clear deprecation path that respects the developers building on the platform. Claude 3 to 3.5 to 4 to 4.5 to Opus 4.6 was not a series of sudden leaps. It was a planned program of reps, in public, refined under load. The market saw the releases, and the team felt the loop.

### Reps With a Purpose

Whether you're logging lifts or logging bugs, the idea is the same: feedback closes the loop. Reps without it just reinforce habits, good or bad. Reps with it are where you find progress.

Athletes track tempo, range, and fatigue. Product teams track usage, drop-off, and latency. Neither discipline rewards brute force for long, but both reward the people who show up, take notes, and come back better.

In the gym, a lifter might swap from high-bar squats to pause squats to work through a sticking point. In product, a team might start dogfooding an unfinished feature to catch friction early. In both cases the rep is the mechanism, and the lesson comes from how you interpret it.

## The Grind That Grows

The reps that change you aren't the glamorous ones. They aren't the PR attempts, the launch keynotes, or the LinkedIn flexes. The reps that build real strength, in the gym and in the product, are the ones nobody sees: the early morning warmups, the late-night bug fixes, the process of showing up, adjusting, refining, and repeating. This is the grind, and it's where the gains are made.

### In the Gym: Warmups, Not Max Outs

You can spot the novices in any weight room, because they go straight for the heaviest lift they can manage. They max out on deadlifts, post it on Instagram, and then disappear for weeks. The ones who stick around warm up, move methodically, hit moderate numbers with perfect form, log their progress, and show up again tomorrow.

Warming up is not a sign of weakness, it is the mark of a seasoned lifter. It is protection, preparation, and part of the process. As Chad Wesley Smith and countless other coaches remind us, progress is built on consistency, not bravado.

### In Product: Quiet Wins That Matter

Product teams fall into the same trap. We celebrate the big splashy features, but the real loyalty comes from the quiet wins: the "minor" UX update that shaves 10 clicks off a daily workflow, the improved loading time, the default that finally makes sense.

We saw this first-hand at Endgame. Yes, we differentiated on high-end protections like in-memory attack prevention, but the reason our champions stayed engaged wasn't just the cool stuff, it was the continuous improvement in how they used the product. With each release we delivered smoother experiences and more intuitive workflows, and it added up.

The same held true at Elastic Security, where we spent over a year deeply focused on the alert triage experience. We met with analysts, we compiled hundreds of hours of feedback, and we made sure everything they needed, the context, the next steps, and a way to act on it, was right at their fingertips. That was not a flashy launch, it was grinding, and it paid off.

> *Not every enhancement gets a press release. But the users notice, and often celebrate, the small, thoughtful changes more than the big ones.*

### What the Grind Teaches

The grind teaches humility, discipline, and attention to detail. It teaches you to care about the user who files enhancement tickets, not just the buyer who signs the deal, and it teaches you to find progress in what looks minor. In product that means tackling the long list of enhancement requests. In lifting it means refining your bar path or your ankle mobility. Both require you to step out of the spotlight and into the discipline of reps.

### The Myth of Motivation

The myth is that motivation carries you. Motivation gets you started, but it won't carry you through the hard sets, the long roadmap, or the friction. What will is mission, ritual, feedback, and community.

The best product managers and lifters don't chase the end state, they chase the process, and somewhere along the way they fall in love with it. That is where mastery begins.

### Look Back to See the Gains

Progress is sneaky. When you're in it every day it's hard to see, which is why lifters take progress pictures, not for vanity but for truth. I've had stretches where I felt completely stuck, only to look at side-by-side photos and realize I had been growing all along.

Product has the same blind spot. One of the best presentations I gave at Endgame was a demo showing the same workflow over multiple years. Each version looked like a small step on its own, but side-by-side it was night and day: better design, better defaults, better detection. That is the power of consistent improvement, and you only see it when you look back.

## Change the Phase, Not the Process

Anyone who's followed a good lifting program knows the feeling: what used to work stops working, you stall, you plateau, you feel stuck. The worst response is to panic and start over. The best response is to adjust the phase and keep the process.

In powerlifting, this is where periodization becomes personal. You might deload, shift your focus to speed or hypertrophy, swap bars, or change your stance, but you don't throw away your structure. You modify it with purpose.

Product development works the same way. You don't abandon the mission when progress slows, you evolve the strategy and change the shape of the work.

### Product Example: Scaling for What's Next

Early in Endgame's life, we took an almost nine-month pause on new features to rearchitect the platform for long-term scale and performance. It was a scary bet, and there were plenty of sleepless nights wondering if it was the right call. But when we brought the new build to a major deployment and watched the blue team "win" their live training exercise for the first time, we knew it had paid off. Their expertise made it happen, and our new platform and agent helped make it possible.

Sometimes changing the phase means giving up momentum now to build strength you can use later. That takes guts, but it's part of the process.

### Lifters Adjust, Too

Smart lifters know when to change gears. That might mean trying a new program, like shifting from 5/3/1 to Juggernaut, or letting an AI-based plan like JuggernautAI retune the work based on fatigue and bar speed. Sometimes it means getting a personal coach, or joining a lifter forum to troubleshoot sticking points. What matters is sticking to the discipline: you're still logging reps and still progressing with intention. The ritual stays and the phase shifts.

Which brings me to the new wrinkle in 2026. AI doesn't just track your reps anymore. It adjusts the plan based on how the bar moved last week, so the program responds to you, and the only thing that can break the loop is whether you show up honest. There is a trade in that, and I'll come back to it in the chapter on AI.

### Set a Goal to Stay Moving

During COVID, lifting alone in my garage, motivation came from an unexpected place: I signed up for a powerlifting meet. I wasn't planning to set any records, but having a date on the calendar gave me focus.

That meet, and the ones that followed, taught me something else. Competition isn't about beating others. It's about beating the weight. The community is there to help you do exactly that, right down to your "opponents" cheering you on.

The same lesson applies in product. When you're feeling stuck, talk to your users and talk to your peers. They'll remind you why you're doing it, and they'll help you find the next rep.

> *Even when you change the phase (the load, the format, the plan), your mission stays, your ritual stays, and the growth continues.*

## Growth That Holds

There's a difference between strength that shows up and strength that holds up, and that difference comes from experience, from failure, and from learning the hard way.

We saw it earlier in the story of Hafthor Björnsson, the world's strongest man, who tore his pec pushing beyond safe limits. He later called it his "biggest mistake" and rebuilt with a smarter plan. The same pattern shows up again and again in high-level lifting. Even Stefi Cohen, one of the most decorated lifters of her generation, has shifted her focus toward long-term health, adjusting how she trains, eats, and recovers, not out of fear but out of wisdom. These lessons apply anywhere high performance matters.

### Product Example: Build Fast, Learn Faster

At Endgame, we learned a critical lesson well before the world-shaking CrowdStrike outage of 2024. We had shipped a powerful capability: the ability to update our machine learning-based malware prevention engine out of cycle from the main product release. It meant our users got faster protection without waiting for a full product upgrade. It was the right feature, and it had an unintended consequence.

During Black Friday at one of our retail customers, an update disrupted point-of-sale systems. Nothing catastrophic, but enough to make an impact. We fixed it fast, added guardrails, changed our rollout process, and never made the same mistake again. More importantly, we kept the feature and improved how it worked for the user, at their scale, in their implementation.

The best product capabilities don't just "work." They hold up. They account for real-world usage, edge cases, and the 80/20 rule, the fact that 80% of your issues will come from 20% of your scenarios, and we built resilience into the system because of it.

### The Product Takeaway

Your best ideas will cause friction if they aren't paired with empathy. It is not enough to be right. You also need to be reliable, and the only way to get there is through reps that test the system, expose the edges, and give you time to learn and evolve. That is how mature products, and mature lifters, endure.
