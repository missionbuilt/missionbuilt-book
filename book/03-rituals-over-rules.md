# 3. Rituals Over Rules

## The Ritual Is the Rail

If the mission is the magnet, the ritual is the rail.

Rituals keep us on track, not by forcing us forward but by guiding our momentum. Like the rails under a train, they don't power the engine, but they make sure we're headed in the right direction, and they keep drift from becoming derailment.

In the gym, the rituals aren't written on the whiteboard, but everyone knows them: the way you chalk your hands before a heavy lift, the nod from your training partner, the slap on the back before a max attempt. None of those moments make you stronger by themselves, but they signal that it's time to lock in, and they tune your mind and body to the work ahead.

In product, rituals shape how we build and why. A startup begins with a weekly sync focused on shipping one differentiated capability. As the company grows, the rituals evolve into structured roadmap reviews, tighter standups, and a shift toward shipping the "boring" features like role-based access control and data retention workflows. RBAC and audit logging will never make the cover of TechCrunch, but they are what your biggest customers need before they can trust you. Those shifts aren't a loss of agility, they are a deepening of commitment, and the rituals grow because the mission does.

The difference between a rule and a ritual is intention. A rule is imposed on you, and it says *"follow this or else."* A ritual is something you earned, and it says *"this is how we move with purpose."* When a team stops asking why it follows a process, that process calcifies into dogma. When the process stays tied to the mission, it adapts, it grows, and it stays human.

Elite lifters know this instinctively. Their programming changes all the time, higher volume, lower intensity, a shift from competition prep to recovery, but their rituals stay intact. The warmup flow, the music, the way they approach the bar all evolve, and none of them ever disappear. They guide the body into readiness and keep the lifter connected to something deeper than the day's numbers.

That's the point. You don't need more rules, you need rails that carry you toward the mission and flex with the turns ahead. The mission is the magnet and the ritual is the rail, and the rail is not there to restrict your path, it is there to keep you from drifting when the pressure builds.

## Listen to Your Signals

Rails only work if you are paying attention to where you are on them, and rituals are no different. You can't run the same program forever, not in the gym, not in product, and not in life. Progress demands feedback, and feedback starts with listening.

Lifters learn this early. You might show up ready to deadlift heavy, but your grip feels off, your back is tight, and your CNS just isn't firing. (CNS is your central nervous system, the part of your body responsible for strength output, coordination, and neural drive. If it is fatigued, you will feel it, even if your muscles are technically rested.) That is not failure, that is information. A smart lifter doesn't abandon the workout, they adjust. They keep the ritual and shift the intensity, maybe pausing at 70%, maybe pivoting to accessories. Listening like that doesn't make you weaker, it keeps you in the game longer.

Product teams learn the same lesson, usually the hard way: rituals without awareness become liabilities. You can run all the ceremonies, sprint planning, retros, standups, but if you are ignoring the signals from your team and your users, you are performing process theater. Burnout doesn't show up in Jira, and disengagement doesn't flash red on a dashboard. You feel it in the delay before someone unmutes, in the tension after a roadmap shift, and in the quiet attrition of both teammates and customers.

Nowhere is this more visible than in the game industry's long-standing reliance on crunch time, the late-stage death marches where teams work 60, 70, 80-hour weeks to hit a ship date. Executives cite passion. Teams call it what it is: avoidable. The rituals of *"just one more sprint,"* of all-hands war rooms, of praise for pulling all-nighters get treated as signs of commitment when they are actually signals of failure: a failure to listen, to plan, to build sustainable systems, and to treat the team as human beings instead of headcount.

This is where product management has a second, often overlooked role. Yes, product is the voice of the user inside the development team. But product is also the shield of the team against the wrong voices from above. A good PM doesn't just absorb pressure from the top and pass it down. A good PM pushes back, uses data to say no to date-driven development, advocates for pacing instead of panic, and protects the team's ability to think, breathe, and build well, even when the deadline is loud.

> *Our goal isn't to meet an investor's timeline or an executive's forecast. It's to build the best product to solve our user's mission.*

And the only way to do that sustainably, meaningfully, and well is to listen, to your body, to your team, to your users, and to the mission.

## Change the Pattern, Not the Practice

Sometimes listening reveals that the ritual itself needs to change, and when things stop working, you don't abandon the ritual, you adapt it. Progress isn't a straight line, it's a cycle of push, plateau, and pivot. When you hit the plateau and the usual ritual no longer delivers results, that is not a sign to quit but a sign to evolve.

In the gym, this happens all the time. You run a successful squat cycle, adding five pounds a week like clockwork, and then you stall. Your knees cave, your speed slows, and the bar starts winning. The answer is not to scrap the movement but to change the pattern. Maybe you shift to pause squats, change your stance, or drop the volume to focus on recovery. The ritual of showing up and squatting stays, and only the shape of it changes.

A product team stalls the same way, and the fix is the same. The standup that once helped your team sync becomes a box-checking chore. The planning meeting that used to set priorities now spirals into status updates. That doesn't mean you stop planning, it means you shift how you plan. You move from live check-ins to async threads, and you replace calendar fatigue with focused, flexible rituals that match what your team needs in its current phase of growth. Rituals that don't evolve become rules, and rules, as we have seen, break people.

This is especially true in globally distributed teams. At Elastic, our workforce spans time zones and continents, and we had to learn early that rituals built for co-located teams don't translate. A sync meeting at 10 a.m. in California is 7 p.m. in Berlin and 2:30 a.m. in Sydney. So we changed the pattern and moved toward asynchronous rhythms:

- Key decisions live in documents, not meetings.
- Slack threads replace sidebars.
- Recorded meetings include transcripts and tagged callouts.
- Big calls are followed by quiet time, space for global teammates to reflect, respond, and contribute.

We didn't stop collaborating, we restructured how and when it happens. The ritual of cross-team communication stayed, its shape changed, and it made space for more voices, not fewer.

We are not the only team that publicly evolved its rituals as it grew. Linear has documented how they shifted from rigid sprints to six-week cycles, lightweight async-first standups, and tighter scope-cutting rituals. The cadence changed and the mission did not. Their team guide reads like a working document of how rituals should evolve as the company evolves around them.

The newest version of this is happening in 2026 with AI. Standups now run with AI-generated summaries instead of live recaps. Retros surface patterns from team data that nobody had the time to read on their own, and mock reviews include an AI co-reviewer. The ritual is still the ritual (show up, learn, adapt), but the form is shifting fast. That is not a problem, it's a sign that rails can take new shapes when the train evolves. I'll come back to what AI changes here, and what it doesn't, in the chapter on AI.

What matters isn't how you do it, it's that you keep showing up with purpose. You hit a wall, you adjust, and you don't abandon the work, you change the pattern. That is the lesson: rituals serve the mission, and when they stop serving it, you reshape them. Don't confuse rigidity for discipline, and don't let the form matter more than the function. (And please, don't treat your favorite product management book like doctrine. Those frameworks weren't written for your team. Your mission was.)

Rituals aren't constraints. They're commitments, but only if we let them evolve. The strongest systems, the longest-lasting teams, and the most resilient lifters all share this in common: they don't cling to routine for routine's sake. They adapt, with intention, with feedback, and with the mission in mind.
