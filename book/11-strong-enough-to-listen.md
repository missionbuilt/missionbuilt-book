# 11. Strong Enough to Listen

> *The hardest lift you will ever make is putting your ego down.*

## Listening as Leadership

In lifting, the strongest athletes don't just follow cues, they go looking for them. They want feedback on form, timing, and control, because they understand that every correction is a chance to get better. That hunger doesn't stop at the gym doors. The best leaders have the same mindset: they don't listen so they can reply. They listen so they can understand.

Listening starts with actually being there. It is easy to nod along while you plan your response. Every leader does it, and every leader who says they don't is doing it right now. The listening that changes anything happens when you quiet your own thoughts long enough to hear what the person across from you actually needs. It might be a training partner describing something that feels off under the bar, or a teammate raising a concern in the middle of a sprint. Either way, the act of listening says: *I value you, and I value what you see.*

We've all dealt with someone who claims to want feedback and then argues or shuts down the moment they get it, right? Or worse, nods along while rehearsing their rebuttal. In lifting, those athletes plateau or get hurt. In leadership the consequences are subtler but just as damaging: honesty fades, the same mistakes repeat, and new ideas stop showing up.

When people decide their leadership won't listen, or watch their feedback get ignored a few times in a row, they stop engaging. Leadership becomes something to avoid rather than something to trust. A conversation with a manager starts to feel like a tax on progress instead of a path to improvement. Silos form as people retreat to wherever they feel heard, or at least left alone.

Feedback exists for a reason. It is an outside perspective on the things we cannot see ourselves. Day after day, stuck in our own heads, we reinforce our own ideas, and some of them are bad ones. Without somebody on the outside telling us, flaws harden into habits.

But feedback has to be given responsibly too. Some people use *"feedback"* as cover for being cruel or condescending. Good leaders know that useful feedback is clear, aimed at the action rather than the person, and delivered with the intent to build rather than break. It is the difference between saying *"You suck at this"* and *"You could improve by keeping your elbows tighter during the press."* The first one deflates somebody. The second one gives them something to do on the next rep.

Earlier in the book I talked about product managers getting lost in tactical execution and missing the bigger picture of the workflow or the user journey. The same thing happens under the bar. You can obsess over the sticking point in your press and never notice that your bar path has been drifting forward the whole time. Stepping back and welcoming constructive feedback is what breaks through those blind spots.

Listening also creates psychological safety, and I have never seen a high-performing team that didn't have it. When people know their ideas and their fears will be heard without judgment, they stop guarding their words and start telling you the truth. That candor is what innovation and resilience grow out of.

Leaders who listen multiply their own perspective. They get dozens of lenses on the mission instead of one, and none of it happens passively. They ask clarifying questions, they invite dissent, and they understand that the best solutions rarely come from a single voice. They come from many voices shaped into one direction.

And just as a lifter has to listen to their own body, leaders have to listen to themselves. Noticing fatigue, frustration, or ego creeping in is the first step to recalibrating. It is leadership turned inward, staying aware of the signals that could compromise a decision or derail the momentum of the team.

> *"Most people do not listen with the intent to understand; they listen with the intent to reply."*
> — Stephen R. Covey, *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*

There will also be days when you screw up. Not every meeting, Slack message, or email is going to land, because you're human. Part of being a good leader is accepting that, taking responsibility for the mistake, and being transparent with your team when it happens. Saying something like *"Yesterday I didn't give you enough time to clearly make your point. I'm sorry. Can we continue the conversation?"* shows strength, not weakness.

Just as empathy is not a sign of softness, openly owning your mistakes builds trust and models the behavior you want from your team. Too many leaders cling to the idea that they have to stay up on a pedestal, and they mistake authority for respect. These explicit-authority leaders demand deference without earning it, forgetting that respect is given when it is first shown. We are all human. Titles are made-up constructs.

> *Be a human, don't be an asshole, and you'll be amazed at how well your team can operate.*

Listening as leadership is a daily discipline. It takes humility, curiosity, the courage to act on what you hear, and the wisdom to give feedback that lifts people up instead of tearing them down. It is the difference between a leader who demands compliance and one who inspires commitment, and between a team that survives and one that thrives.

## Listening Between the Lines

Good leaders listen to the words, and great leaders listen past them, because what people say rarely tells the whole story. The subtext often tells you more than the words do: the hesitation in a voice, the glance across the table, the silence after a loaded question.

In lifting, an athlete might tell you they're fine while shifting around uncomfortably under the bar. You can hear it in their breathing, see it in a setup that wavers, or sense it in the way they rack the bar with relief instead of resolve. A coach who misses those cues either pushes an athlete toward an injury or lets a lack of confidence fester unaddressed.

The same thing plays out at work. A teammate says *"I'm good with this plan"* while their tone flattens or their posture closes off. They contribute less in meetings, and their messages get shorter and less engaged. Those are signals a leader has to learn to read, because they're the early warnings of misalignment, burnout, or resentment starting to build.

Listening between the lines is not mind-reading. It is a set of techniques called active listening that help you tune into more than the words:

- **Observe nonverbal cues.** Pay attention to body language, facial expressions, eye contact, posture, and gestures. Discomfort, stress, or disagreement often shows up physically before it's spoken.
- **Reflect and paraphrase.** Repeat back what you heard in your own words. It shows you're tracking, and it gives the other person a chance to clarify or expand. For example, *"So you're saying the timeline feels tight, but you think it's still doable?"*
- **Ask open-ended questions.** Questions like *"How do you feel about this approach?"* or *"What would you change?"* invite honest responses and help surface unspoken concerns.
- **Pause intentionally.** Silence can feel uncomfortable, but giving space after someone speaks often encourages them to share more, especially when they sense you're patient enough to truly listen.
- **Listen for tone and pace.** Speeding up, slowing down, or changes in pitch can signal anxiety, excitement, or doubt.
- **Eliminate distractions.** Close your laptop, put your phone away, and make eye contact. Active listening demands full presence, and split attention sends the message that what they're saying isn't important.

These techniques help you catch the gap between the words and the intent. That gives you a much clearer picture of what your team needs.

None of this is theoretical. When Alan Mulally became CEO of Ford in 2006, he started "Business Plan Review" meetings where executives reported on their areas with green, yellow, or red indicators. Ford was losing billions at the time, and at first everyone reported green anyway. Mulally noticed the nervous laughter, the fidgeting, and the avoidance, clear signs that people were afraid to share bad news. He called it out. The next week an executive showed a red indicator. Mulally praised the honesty publicly, and the culture of fear broke almost overnight. He read the gap between what people said and how they behaved. That changed Ford's culture, surfaced the real problems, and helped the company avoid bankruptcy without a government bailout.

A similar lesson comes from the boxing ring. During the 2020 fight between Deontay Wilder and Tyson Fury, Wilder's corner threw in the towel in the seventh round even though Wilder insisted he wanted to keep fighting. His coach, Mark Breland, saw the subtle signs: Wilder's legs were unsteady, his reflexes had dulled, and his punches had lost their snap. Those were clear indicators that he was in real danger. Breland's choice was controversial, but many praised him for protecting Wilder from a potentially devastating injury. He acted on what he saw, not just on what his fighter said, and put the athlete's long-term health ahead of pride in the moment.

When you can spot misalignment early, you can address it before it turns into conflict or disengagement. When you can spot uncertainty, you can offer clarity. And when you show someone that you see past their words, you prove you care enough to truly understand them.

AI can hear what gets said. It cannot hear what gets meant. I will come back to that gap in the chapter on AI.

## The Ego Lift

Some leaders believe the biggest threat to success comes from the outside: competitors, market shifts, resource constraints. I think the greatest danger to a team is usually internal: an unchecked ego at the top. Ego convinces leaders that they alone have the answers, blinds them to new information, and makes them resistant to change. It turns what should be a shared mission into a personal crusade and suffocates the creativity and collaboration a team needs to adapt.

The ego lift shows up when a leader believes their title grants them absolute knowledge, or when an athlete refuses coaching because they're sure they know best. Those moments look like confidence, but they're insecurity dressed up as bravado, and they poison teams faster than almost anything else.

Listening doesn't work when ego is standing in the way. A leader might nod along waiting for their turn to speak, or worse, interrupt to redirect the conversation back to themselves. Either way the effect is the same: people stop sharing, meetings become performative, feedback withers, silos grow, and the whole organization slows down.

This is the dark mirror of what I described at the end of the last chapter. I mean the leaders who claim they alone have the vision and force alignment through dictation instead of inspiration. They believe respect comes from authority rather than credibility. These explicit-authority leaders declare a path without inviting any discussion, and they mistake fear for alignment. Under that kind of command, teams comply on the outside while disengaging on the inside, working to avoid conflict rather than to achieve anything great.

It's the leadership equivalent of *"because I said so."* Seniority doesn't always mean smarter. Experience brings real insight and intuition, but it is not infallible, and a fresh perspective can break a stale pattern. The surgical teams who learned patient handovers from Formula 1 pit crews saved lives because the mission mattered more than their egos.

Leadership also never stops changing, so it demands continuous learning. In *What Got You Here Won't Get You There*, Marshall Goldsmith describes how the traits that once fueled a leader's success can turn into blind spots if the leader fails to grow. Markets change, requirements shift, and what worked in a previous era cannot simply be applied wholesale to new problems. Those past lessons are still critical for growth, they just need to be treated as guides rather than rigid blueprints.

The founder's dilemma captures this perfectly. Founders are often great at the vision, the hustle, and the risk-taking it takes to start a company. As the organization grows, though, the same command-and-control habits or unstructured creativity that fueled the early wins can become obstacles. The company starts to need process, delegation, and collaborative leadership. When founders cling to the old ways, convinced their instincts alone will scale the business, they risk undermining the very mission they set out to achieve. The founders who make it through recognize the shift and evolve. They seek out mentors, build diverse teams, and accept that their role has to change if the company is going to thrive past the startup stage.

When everyone agrees the mission is the most important thing, these decisions get easier. Reaching out for help or inviting a different viewpoint is the obvious choice when it serves the mission. People who won't listen or won't try are usually acting from fear. More often than not it's a personal agenda driving them, not the team's success. Product management has no place for ladder-climbers or title-chasers. The mission must come first, and when it is your guiding light, every other decision becomes clearer.

When a team truly cares about the mission, ego fades. In a satisfying paradox, personal success tends to follow anyway, as a symptom of the mission's success.

> *The hardest lift you will ever make is putting your ego down.*

The strongest teams are led by people who do not let their ego, their vanity, or their fear get in the way.
