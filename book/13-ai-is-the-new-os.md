# 13. AI Is the New OS. The Mission Is the Only App That Matters.

This is the chapter you have been reading toward, because across the previous twelve chapters AI has shown up at the edges of almost every principle in this book. In Chapter 2 it could do the boring reps for you, though the reps that grow you still belong to whoever shows up willing to feel them, and in Chapter 6 it became the teammate that doesn't sleep, doesn't deload, and doesn't take Q4 off, which made recovery a question of when to walk away from output that is always available. In Chapter 8 it changed what a stress test looks like, from the months-long shape of Log4j to attackers moving in seconds, and in Chapter 10 it joined the team. In Chapter 11 it raised a question we were not ready to answer, whether a tool that can hear what gets said can hear what gets meant, and in Chapter 12 it became the most dangerous line in any review meeting: *"the AI did it."*

This is where those threads converge. It is not a defense of AI or an attack on it. It is an honest test of whether this book's principles still hold when the work itself is changing under our feet.

The motto that runs this chapter is the same one that runs the rest of the book: *Give a shit.* I planted it in the prologue and named its source there, VMM-364, the Purple Foxes, the Marine tiltrotor squadron whose pilots fly CASEVAC into enemy fire to bring wounded troops home. I traced it through Nate Fick at Endgame in Chapter 1, and I lived a version of it in my own Army intel work in Chapter 8. Now I have to test it against the loudest tool of the year I finished writing this book, twice: once under the bar, and once in the boardroom.

---

## AI Under the Bar

I program with JuggernautAI. It watches my sets, learns from them, and reprograms when it needs to. Fatigue shows up and the plan adjusts. I tweak a hamstring and it backs me off, I hit a PR and it pushes harder next cycle. It is the closest thing to a real coach in your pocket that has ever existed, and it does not lift the bar for me.

That is the part most people miss. They hear *"AI program"* and assume the work just got easier, when really it got smarter and the responsibility shifted onto the lifter.

The whole system runs on one input: the truth. How many reps did I really have left? Did I actually hit depth, or did I cheat the angle because the camera was off? Did I sleep, or did I tell the app I slept? AI cannot guess. It can only respond to what I tell it. If I lie to the app, the program lies back to me, and if I show up honest, it finds me ground I would not have found alone. That is the trade: AI gives you a coach, and you give AI the truth.

---

Here is what I have come to believe: AI does not replace the work so much as it exposes the people who were never doing the work in the first place. For years, getting a real strength program meant one of two things. Either you could afford a coach you trusted, who knew your body and would adjust your block when your knee was angry, or you got lucky with a template online and were disciplined enough to follow it without modification. Most lifters never had either. They followed something they read in a forum in 2014, never deloaded, never adjusted volume around life, and trained for years without moving their numbers. They were doing the reps, but they were not really training.

That is the failure AI just removed, and it was a failure of access, not effort. A good AI program is not magically better than a great human coach who knows you, watches you move, and pushes you at the right moment, that is still the best version of this work. But great coaches are rare, expensive, and hard to vet, and most of the people lifting in garage gyms and corporate gyms and basement bars are never going to find one. AI did not beat the great coach. It made expert-level programming available to the lifter who was never going to find one. That is what democratized expertise actually means: not the death of the coach, but the end of the gatekeeping.

---

We love to see the numbers go up, that is why we lift. The dynamic, expert-led program is what makes them go up, and AI just put it in everyone's pocket. The lifter who plateaued for three years following a ten-year-old PDF can now run a program that actually responds to them. The dad training in his garage at 5 a.m. between school runs gets feedback he would never have paid for in cash. The fifty-year-old coming back from injury gets a return-to-load curve that no textbook would have written for their body. The floor of the work just got higher, and the ceiling is still where it has always been, as far as you are willing to grind for it.

---

Here is what AI does not change. The bar still bends under load. The night before a heavy set still keeps you up, and the platform still asks the question it has always asked: *what do you actually have, today?* AI cannot answer that for you. It can only set the stage, and the lift is still yours.

*Give a shit* is the working motto of my career, the one I traced in the prologue and Chapter 1 from VMM-364 through Nate Fick, and AI does not give a shit. That part has not changed and will not. Hitting depth still requires you to give a shit. The PR still requires you to want it, and the grind still requires you to feel it. AI is the program. Giving a shit is the lifter.

So if you are using AI to train, three things are now your job and only yours, three forms of giving a shit under the bar.

**Show up.** Every day, even when the spark fades, and especially then.

**Tell the truth.** To the app, to your training log, and most of all to yourself. Two reps in reserve means two, not one and a maybe.

**Own the lift.** The program did not squat the weight. You did, so the PR is yours, the miss is yours, and the progress is yours.

That is the trade AI offers under the bar: a smarter program, in exchange for a lifter who still gives a shit. It is the best deal strength training has ever seen, but only if you take it seriously.

## AI in the Boardroom

I run product with Claude as a teammate. It reads my epics and asks me what I missed. It builds wireframes from a written brief in minutes, summarizes a competitor's last six press releases before my coffee is cold, and drafts sections of a roadmap I would have spent an evening on. It is the closest thing to a senior editor and a junior designer and a research analyst in your pocket that has ever existed, and it does not feel what the user feels.

That is the seam in this whole conversation. The discourse around AI in product management has been quick to declare the role obsolete. Engineers can prompt their way to an epic, AI can summarize a customer call, and anyone with the right tools can ship a working prototype before lunch. The argument goes: if all of that is true, what does the PM still do?

*Robotic* is already the word we use when a person responds without empathy. *"That answer was robotic."* *"Don't be so robotic with the customer."* It is not a compliment when we apply it to a human, so why would it be the goal for how products get built? We do not want robots building experiences. We need empathy, we need relation, and we need a human.

That motto, *give a shit*, is the line. A model can render empathy convincingly and mimic the language of caring, but it cannot live it and it cannot actually care. The Purple Foxes flew into enemy fire because someone on the other end was bleeding. That is not a workflow or a prompt. That is what humans do for other humans when nothing else will do, it is also what product work at its best is, and it is the part AI is not crossing.

---

The whole system runs on one input here too: presence. Did you actually sit with the user, or did you let AI summarize the call? Did you watch them try the workflow, or did you ask the model what frustrated them? Did you carry their problem home with you, or did you tag the recording and move on?

AI cannot guess, it can only respond to what you give it. Give it your assumptions and it gives back a confidently written version of your assumptions. Give it a real problem you have actually witnessed and it gives back something worth building on. The difference between a PM who is good with AI and one who is great is whether they have actually been in the room. That is the trade, same as under the bar: AI gives you a teammate, and you give AI your time with the user.

---

I believe the same thing here as under the bar: AI does not replace the PM so much as it exposes the people who were never doing the empathetic work in the first place.

Melissa Perri, who has trained more product leaders than almost anyone in the field, frames it cleanly: *AI excels at the how. You own the why.* That is the right line. AI is a stunningly good *how* engine. Specs, drafts, competitive scans, design variations, prototype builds, and edge-case enumeration all come out faster, cheaper, and more thorough than what a human alone produced two years ago. But the *why* has not moved. The user, the mission, the right problem to solve in the first place: that is still a question only a human in the room with another human can answer.

This is also why the LinkedIn experiment is worth taking seriously. Tomer Cohen, LinkedIn's Chief Product Officer, has been replacing traditional PM roles with what he calls *Full Stack Builders*, generalists who design, prompt, prototype, and ship end to end with AI rather than handing off across functions. The structural argument is sound. The role boundary is doing less work than it used to, and I do not disagree with Cohen on the mechanics.

But the question is not whether you call the role PM or full-stack builder. The question is whether the person doing the work can feel what the user feels. A full-stack builder who can do that is a product creator, and one who cannot is shipping faster mediocrity in a smaller org chart. The title is downstream of the empathy.

Marty Cagan has made this point for years, and AI made it urgent. The title PM is not protected, the work of *product creation* is. People in product manager roles who never developed product sense are vulnerable, and people in any role who can sit with a user, name the real problem, and own the outcome are the ones who matter. AI raised the floor on execution, not on judgment.

---

The PM's epic is changing because of this. A great epic in 2026 should be readable to AI. It should explain the problem clearly enough that an AI editor can ask sharp questions back, describe the experience cleanly enough that an AI prototyper can wire a working version, and position competitively in language an AI strategist can stress-test. The best PMs I work with use that as their bar. If I uploaded this epic to Claude, would the AI understand the problem? Would it know what experience solves it? Would it know what competitors are doing nearby? When the answer is yes, you have written a real epic. When the answer is no, you have written a paragraph that hides what you do not yet understand.

There is a catch, though. Pawel Brodzinski, who writes about this more clearly than most, names a structural truth the discourse keeps missing: AI by definition pulls toward the median. The model produces what the data's center of gravity says is most likely, and creativity lives at the fringes of the distribution. So an AI-generated epic is by definition a median epic: confident, clean, and average. The PM's job is to be the deviation that finds the actual user insight the median was never going to surface. AI gives you a faster median, and the human is the difference between median and meaningful.

---

What AI gives the PM in 2026 is access. The PM at a five-person startup can produce at the pace of a PM at Stripe. The PM with three product surfaces can keep up with all three, and the new PM ships in their first month at a level that used to take a year to learn. The floor of the work just got higher here too, and the ceiling has not moved an inch: it is still as far as you are willing to sit with a user and care about their problem.

---

Here is what AI does not change. The customer still wants someone who sees them. The engineering team still needs a clear *why*, the roadmap still has to make a choice, and the launch still has to be owned. The user still wants to know there is a human on the other side of the product who took their mission seriously. AI cannot do that part, it can only set the stage.

So if you are running product with AI, three things are now your job and only yours, the same three forms of giving a shit, in the boardroom.

**Show up.** In the room with the user, not the transcript, and on the call, not in the summary. AI cannot replace your time. It can only multiply it.

**Tell the truth.** About what you actually know versus what you assumed, about what the data says versus what your gut says when they conflict, and about what your team can ship versus what AI made look easy.

**Own the outcome.** You signed off on it, so you own it. The most dangerous line in any review meeting, the one I named in Chapter 12, is *"the AI did it."* The agent did not write the spec alone and the model did not pick the customer to ship to. AI is a teammate, and like any teammate it can let you down, but the responsibility to finish does not transfer.

That is the trade AI offers in the boardroom: a faster team, in exchange for a PM who still gives a shit. It is the best deal product management has ever seen. The catch is that the deal is only good for someone who can still feel what the user feels, who still carries their problem home, and who still wants to be the human they meet on the other side of the product. Stay that person and AI makes you better at the job. Lose that person and AI just helps you ship faster to people you stopped seeing.

---

Those are two halves of one argument. AI raised the floor of training under the bar and the floor of execution in the boardroom. In neither case did it raise the ceiling of the work that matters. The lift is still yours, the user is still theirs, and the mission is still the only thing worth running on top of any of it.

There is a metaphor from my published work that belongs here. AI is the Iron Man suit. The suit makes you faster, stronger, and harder to kill, but it does not tell you what is worth fighting for, or who you are fighting for, or when to take the suit off and look someone in the eye. The human inside the suit is still the only one who can answer those questions. The suit changed everything about the work. The mission inside the suit did not move at all.

---

The second metaphor, also from my published work, names what is structurally happening: AI is the new operating system. The interface layer is shifting from applications to AI environments, and the conversation is becoming the workspace. Everything that used to be an app, your dashboard, your roadmap tool, your customer interview, your training log, is becoming an app inside an AI environment that stitches them together. That is real, and it is also incomplete.

An operating system on its own runs nothing. It needs an app, and there is one app AI cannot build, ship, debug, or maintain on its own: the mission, the decision about who you are building for, and why, and what you owe them. That decision belongs to a human. It always has, and it still does.

AI is the new OS.

The mission is the only app that matters.

And the only thing that runs the app is a person who gives a shit.

---

That is the argument of this chapter, and of the year I added it. The Drift will show up here too. We named it three times in this book: mission overridden by metric in Chapter 1, sprinting without recovery in Chapter 6, and the legacy-shaped Drift in Chapter 12. The AI-shaped Drift, the fourth one we have now lived with long enough to see clearly, looks like a team that has stopped asking *why* because the *how* has become so frictionless. Why interview the user when AI summarized their last call? Why agonize over the brief when AI will refine it? Why slow down to feel the user's problem when AI can render it for you in plausible English? That is the Drift in its 2026 shape, the same shape as the other three, with the same antidote: the mission, the user, and the motto.

Give a shit. About the work. About the people. About the outcome. That is the only app that matters.

Now go run it.
