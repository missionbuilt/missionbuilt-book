# 7. Train the Engine, Not Just the Output

## Work Capacity Over Window Dressing

There is a difference between looking strong and being strong, and you see it in the gym all the time. The lifter who chases a one-rep max every week, treating PRs like progress reports, looks impressive until the plateaus hit or the pain sets in. What you don't see as often is the lifter dragging a sled after squats, doing walking lunges in the cold, rowing in the morning and hitting accessories at night. That is the engine work, and that is capacity. If you want to go far, build the lungs before the legs.

In the training world this is called GPP, General Physical Preparedness, and it is more than a warmup or conditioning. It is the foundation everything else sits on. GPP improves recovery between sets and sessions. It builds joint integrity, tissue resilience, and work tolerance, and it makes your system ready to train harder, recover faster, and adapt more consistently. And yet in a lot of strength circles it gets dismissed. You'll hear things like:

- *"Combo sets are my cardio."*
- *"I don't have time for fluff. Just the main lifts."*
- *"If it doesn't add pounds to the bar, it's a distraction."*

That is the specialization trap, and it is seductive, because specialized programs promise fast results. Just do the thing you want to improve, more of it. It works until it doesn't, until your joints hurt, your energy fades, your momentum disappears, and you burn out.

Repetition without variation isn't just physically risky, it is psychologically draining. Even the most disciplined lifter starts dreading the next workout, and even the most motivated developer begins to disengage. I've seen this in teams that only work on performance, or only ship new features. The work becomes mechanical, morale suffers, curiosity fades, and the team ends up strong in one motion but brittle under real-world load.

That is why smart teams, and smart lifters, build in variation. Take the 6/2 cycle popularized by Basecamp: six weeks of deep, focused work followed by two weeks of cooldown. That is capacity training, not a break. We looked at it in Chapter 6, and other companies have since adopted and evolved it because it works. GPP isn't glamorous, but it is what lets you train hard today and show up strong again tomorrow.

Alex Viada understood that. A biomedical engineer and hybrid athlete, Viada rejected the false binary between strength and endurance. He trained for both, and he thrived. While most lifters feared that conditioning would eat their gains, Viada built an aerobic base big enough to carry heavy squats and sub-5:00 miles. His results weren't just impressive, they were repeatable and durable.

You see the same principle in product teams. Linear didn't try to dazzle with a full feature suite out of the gate. Instead they trained their engine, performance: every click, every transition, every load state was built to feel instant, even offline. That invisible investment became their advantage. Users stayed focused, fast, and frictionless, and while competitors stacked features, Linear scaled loyalty.

The obsession with output can take an even darker turn, though, in the gym and in the market. Some lifters now chase not even performance but the appearance of performance. Injections like synthol or site-enhancing oils artificially inflate muscle volume in the biceps, shoulders, and traps to create the illusion of strength, with no increase in output or health, just ballooned tissue under the skin. These are vanity muscles, pure and simple, and in too many cases they don't just harm the lifter's reputation. They destroy the lifter's body, through heart failure, sepsis, amputation, and in some cases death.

> *When looking strong matters more than being strong, the mission is already lost.*

Product teams do the same thing with vanity metrics, injecting them into roadmaps: flashy dashboards no one uses, AI features built for the pitch deck, slideware that outpaces software. It might look good in the moment, but behind the scenes the engine is breaking down. I'll come back to the AI version of this in the chapter on AI, because when the model can generate any output you ask it for, the question of what is actually under the hood becomes the only one that matters.

You can fake a feature and you can fake a max lift, but you can't fake the foundation. Burnout is what happens when you try to specialize your way out of exhaustion. Capacity is what happens when you train for the long haul. The ones who last don't just train the output, they train the system behind it.

## Internal Strength for External Load

Strength isn't just about what you can lift. It's about what you can withstand.

Anyone can show up strong once. The mission demands more than that: it demands repeatability, resilience, and the ability to show up strong again and again, under pressure, under uncertainty, under load. That kind of strength doesn't come from testing yourself every day. It comes from building yourself for the test, from the inside out.

Lifters learn this the hard way. If you neglect your stabilizers, your joints, your breath control, your bracing, it doesn't matter what your numbers look like. You'll break. The biggest total in the gym means nothing if your back gives out the next week, because true strength is not just the output, it is the integrity of the system supporting it. The best lifters I know don't chase the max, they train the margin.

Amanda Lawrence understands that. A multi-time world champion powerlifter, Amanda is famous not just for her records, squatting over 600 pounds raw at 84kg, but for her poise under pressure. She visualizes every meet lift weeks in advance, rehearses the rituals, and manages recovery like a professional sport. She trains her nervous system, her mental process, and her ability to stay composed under load, not just her muscles, and that internal strength, not quad size or grip, is what lets her hit massive lifts on the world stage even when the bar bends and the cameras are rolling.

### They Trained the Tendons of the Product, Not Just the Muscle

In product, that internal strength is the architecture nobody sees, and Notion is the example I reach for. Notion wasn't always a darling. In 2015 they were nearly dead, with no traction and technical debt piling up. The team had two choices: patch the pain and push forward, or stop, go dark, and rebuild the core from scratch.

They chose the hard route. Two engineers rewrote the entire editor engine over 18 months, with no releases and no hype, just deep technical investment in a stronger, more flexible system. They bet that the internal strength of the product, not surface-level features, would determine their long-term viability, and they were right. That rebuilt core became the foundation for everything that followed: blocks, embeds, team permissions, scalable collaboration. The invisible architecture made possible everything the user eventually saw. What saved Notion wasn't a sprint, it was a rebuild.

Throughout my career I've watched scale and stability work get dismissed as unexciting, not flashy enough for slides and not "pressable" enough for headlines. Some leaders fall into the trap of always needing something new to talk about, something shippable that demos well. That pressure leads to bloated products, incoherent strategies, and worst of all, users who suffer when the product finally meets real-world load. When everything matters most, the engine breaks.

Capability refinement is not the boring work, it is the hard work, the place where the most difficult engineering problems live and where the most intricate, high-stakes user requirements get applied, not in the pitch deck but in the heat of usage.

Building a reliable core doesn't mean every startup needs to design for a Fortune 10 on day one. It does mean carving out time in the roadmap to move deliberately in that direction, and asking the hard questions early. Not just *"What are we building?"* but *"Who are we building it for?"* and, just as critically, *"Who are we not building it for?"* You cannot build for every user in every vertical in every geo all at once. Trying leads to brittle systems and vague products that serve no one well.

Laser focus is strength. Define your product requirements, define your user, define the scale, define your edge cases and your tests, and then do the work, especially the work that doesn't make the press release. That is where real durability comes from, not the big reveal but the boring reps.

### A Story I've Lived

In July 2016, as I described in the prologue, my engineering lead and I stood on the floor of the Combined Air Operations Center at Nellis, watching our agent run through a live Air Force exercise. The story behind that moment starts earlier.

Early at Endgame, after CEO Nate Fick made the difficult but necessary decision to narrow our product portfolio to one core offering, engineering leader Tony Meehan and I found ourselves at a crossroads. The product we had was powerful. It delivered real results, and we had major enterprise deals on the horizon. But the architecture wasn't built for scale, and the team had shown clearly that it wouldn't hold as we moved into larger opportunities.

We could have coasted, closed the deals, and deferred the hard part. But we knew that if we built a base of users on that foundation, the eventual transition would be expensive and painful. So in early 2016 we made the call to rebuild. We moved the front end to a more modern language, redesigned the back end with a new queuing architecture, and rewrote how the endpoint agent communicated with the platform. All of it had to be done in under six months, alongside new features, for a critical delivery with the U.S. Department of Defense.

It was brutal: long hours, hard conversations, and constant doubt. But standing in that live DoD exercise, watching operators succeed with what we had built, it was clear the effort had been worth it. The new architecture held. It scaled, and it supported the mission until we joined Elastic in 2019.

> *Internal strength isn't flashy. But it's what gets you through the weight that would break someone else.*

## What Powers You?

The fastest engines in the world still go nowhere without fuel. You can have systems as good as anyone's, perfect technique, and an unbreakable schedule, but without something driving you, a reason to push through when it's hard and a mission that makes the effort matter, the engine stalls. Strength without fuel is just potential.

This is the third layer of performance. After physical preparation and mental resilience comes purpose, the why behind the work, the force that powers the engine when the load gets heavy and the results are still a long way off.

You can see the difference on the platform. Some lifters are chasing numbers. Others are lifting for something deeper: to test themselves, to prove something to a younger version of who they were, or to inspire someone watching from the crowd. The same weight moves differently depending on what's driving it. Product teams move the same way, and the ones that ship fastest aren't just well organized. They're aligned around a purpose they care about. They understand the mission, they know what their work means to the people who use it, and that makes them more resilient, not just more productive.

> *When the fuel runs low, purpose drives on.*

Take Kristy Hawkins. Before she was a record-breaking powerlifter, she was a biochemical engineer at Genentech, and whether in a lab or under a barbell she trains with the same energy source: a relentless drive to understand, improve, and own her progress. She's powered by process, not comparison, and that is what makes her strength sustainable.

Teams run on the same fuel. Look at how Superhuman built their product. Founder Rahul Vohra didn't just want to make a faster inbox, he wanted to build the fastest, most delightful email experience ever created, and they didn't launch until users said they'd be *"very disappointed"* if they lost the product. That wasn't marketing polish, it was purpose, measured. Every decision, from UI transitions to onboarding flow, was shaped by that internal fire: delight, clarity, joy. The product knew what it was, and the team knew why it mattered.

Capacity is mechanical and mental strength is adaptive, but purpose is emotional. It's fuel, which is why it matters what you're building strength for. You can build a massive work capacity and still burn out if you're not connected to what it's in service of. Even strong people lose their way when they forget why they're moving in the first place. The question isn't only whether you can go further, it's why you would.

Ask any seasoned lifter what keeps them going and the answers get quieter over time. It isn't the meet or the total, it's that the ritual grounds them, makes them better, and reminds them of who they are. Ask a product team what keeps them shipping when everything breaks, and if they're still smiling, it's because the mission means something to them. They've felt the user's pain, they've seen the real-world impact, and they've tasted the outcome of building something that matters.

So, what powers you? Not just when things are working and people are watching, but when the bar is stuck, when the sprint is a slog, when the road is long and there's no applause in sight.

That is the kind of test SERE training is designed for. In the U.S. military's Survival, Evasion, Resistance, and Escape course, people are pushed to their psychological limits: deprived of sleep, isolated, interrogated. It is a test of skill, but more than that it is a test of resolve, of remembering the mission when the environment is engineered to make you forget. The ones who endure don't have better gear or more talent. They have clarity, and they know what they're fighting for.

That is your fuel. That is your engine. That is the difference between showing up when it's easy and showing up when it counts.
